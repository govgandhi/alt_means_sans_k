\begin{abstract}
Random-walk network embeddings assign each node a direction and a norm, whose structural meaning depends on the population operator, realized graph, and training.
We compare the population geometry of normalized spectral embeddings, random-walk pair probabilities, and skip-gram with negative sampling under the degree-corrected stochastic block model in a common notation.
The first two operators have an exact radial-angular structure: degree propensity controls radius within each community, while direction depends only on community.
At unigram negative sampling, however, marginal normalization cancels node-specific degree from the optimal skip-gram score.
We then use controlled finite-sample experiments to probe sensitivity to exposure and context sampling.
Equal exposure leaves norms decreasing with degree, proportional exposure makes them increase, and exposure proportional to the cube root of frequency produces an inverted-U.
Mixing node contexts toward their marginal distribution removes the decreasing branch.
Corrected word2vec controls show that turnover persists without subsampling under the original learning-rate schedule, but is absent at twenty epochs under a constant-rate alternative.
We then freeze predictions based only on realized pair exposure and empirical context KL before training embeddings on two new synthetic graph families and the NetScience coauthorship network.
Across nine graph-family--threshold curves, the median peak error is zero degree bins, eight peaks lie within one bin of their predictions, and the pooled node-level Spearman correlation is \(0.881\).
A post hoc degree-and-threshold baseline also passes the declared criteria, but predicts within-curve node-norm rankings less accurately.
The controlled curves are consistent with a weakening norm response to exposure relative to context specificity; exposure itself need not decrease or reach a fixed ceiling.
An exact graph construction shows that arbitrarily small objective gaps can also permit distinct balanced norm-degree shapes after removing overall scale.
These results distinguish population geometry, realized-graph targets, and trained norms, providing testable controls for interpreting radial information in network embeddings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Random-walk embedding methods map nodes to vectors by sampling paths through a network and training a predictive model on the resulting node sequences~\citep{perozzi2014deepwalk,grover2016node2vec}.
Most applications compare vector directions through cosine similarity.
The vector norm is nevertheless a second observable that can encode node frequency, contextual concentration, and training dynamics.
Its interpretation matters whenever embeddings enter a dot product, a clustering method without row normalization, or a downstream model that retains vector scale.

Empirical work has reported a nonmonotonic relation between frequency and embedding norm.
Word2vec norms first increase and then decrease with word frequency in domain-specific corpora~\citep{schakel2015measuring,wilson2015controlled}.
Trajectory embeddings of scientific organizations show the same inverted-U against organization size: norms increase up to roughly \(10^3\) researchers and then decrease for larger institutions~\citep{murray2023unsupervised}.
The proposed explanation is a competition between repeated exposure, which pushes an embedding away from its initialization, and context diversity, which makes the update directions less coherent.
A population account must distinguish the graph-conditioned target from training dynamics and specify what each intervention changes.

Two theoretical traditions address parts of the problem.
Population spectral embeddings under the degree-corrected stochastic block model (DCSBM) have community rays, with node-specific length multipliers in SCORE~\citep{jin2015fast} and regularized spectral clustering~\citep{qin2013regularized}.
The matrix-factorization view of skip-gram identifies a shifted pointwise mutual information score~\citep{levy2014neural,qiu2018network}, while an information-geometric analysis relates a centered, transformed word-vector norm to the Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence between conditional and marginal context distributions~\citep{oyama2022norm}.
Related analyses establish sampler-dependent targets~\citep{davison2023asymptotics}, including a block-constant DCSBM target at unigram negative sampling~\citep{davison2024community}.
These results do not by themselves determine the norms returned by finite stochastic optimization.

We compare these population results with finite training.
Under the DCSBM, the normalized-adjacency eigenspace and the stationary random-walk pair matrix have exact radial-angular factorizations.
The radial exponent is \(1/2\) for the normalized eigenspace and \(1\) for raw stationary pairs.
The optimal skip-gram score behaves differently: unigram negative sampling cancels node-specific degree, and other negative-sampling exponents add a context-side log-degree term.
Because skip-gram identifies dot products rather than either factor, individual norms require a balancing convention or an explicit optimizer.

We then vary exposure and conditional contexts in heterogeneous configuration-model networks.
The exposure intervention assigns each node an exact number of positive-center updates proportional to stationary frequency raised to an exponent \(\beta\).
The context intervention mixes each conditional context distribution toward its own marginal, preserving that marginal before integer pair allocation.
Equal exposure reveals a decreasing norm-degree relation; proportional exposure produces a monotone increase; the exponent \(1/3\) produces an inverted-U, and \(2/3\) still produces an increase.
Context mixing removes the decreasing branch.
Corrected Gensim controls reproduce the peak movement with weaker subsampling under the original learning-rate schedule, but also show that the unfiltered curve depends on learning-rate history.
Before training on new targets, we freeze node-level norms and peak locations predicted from realized positive-pair exposure and context KL.
The predictions reproduce the peak movement in direct preferential-attachment graphs, clustered scale-free graphs, and an empirical coauthorship network, with explicit misses at the boundaries of the tested degree range.

Our random-walk formulas apply to DeepWalk, equivalently node2vec with return and in--out parameters \(p=q=1\).
The SGNS results assume negative sampling in place of the hierarchical softmax of the original DeepWalk~\citep{perozzi2014deepwalk}.
General node2vec walks are second-order Markov chains on directed edges and require a separate state-space argument.

\section{DCSBM setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Let \(n\) nodes belong to \(K\) communities.
Write \(g_i\in\{1,\dots,K\}\) for the community of node \(i\), and let \(Z\in\{0,1\}^{n\times K}\) be the membership matrix.
Each node has a degree propensity \(\theta_i>0\), collected in \(\Theta=\diag(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_n)\).
Let \(B\in\R^{K\times K}\) be symmetric with nonnegative entries.
We assume that every community is nonempty, that the block degree factors defined below are positive, and that \(\theta_i\theta_jB_{g_i g_j}\leq 1\).

The rank-\(K\) population proxy is
\begin{equation}
    \Omega=\Theta ZBZ^\top\Theta,
    \qquad
    \Omega_{ij}=\theta_i\theta_jB_{g_i g_j}.
    \label{eq:omega}
\end{equation}
This is the standard multiplicative DCSBM structure~\citep{karrer2011stochastic}.

Define the block propensity masses and block degree factors by
\begin{equation}
    s_a=\sum_{i:g_i=a}\theta_i,
    \qquad
    \kappa_a=(Bs)_a=\sum_{b=1}^K B_{ab}s_b.
    \label{eq:block-masses}
\end{equation}
The population expected degree is
\begin{equation}
    \delta_i=(\Omega\ones)_i=\theta_i\kappa_{g_i}.
    \label{eq:expected-degree}
\end{equation}
Let \(\mathcal{D}=\diag(\delta_1,\dots,\delta_n)\) and
\begin{equation}
    \vol(\Omega)
    :=\ones^\top\Omega\ones
    =\sum_i\delta_i
    =s^\top Bs.
    \label{eq:volume}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}[Diagonal convention]
Equation~\eqref{eq:omega} includes the diagonal of the low-rank population proxy.
A loopless DCSBM has mean adjacency
\(\Omega-\diag(\Omega)\).
If we retain the proxy normalization \(\mathcal{D}\), removing the diagonal changes the normalized operator by a diagonal matrix with operator norm
\begin{equation}
    \max_i \frac{\theta_i B_{g_i g_i}}{\kappa_{g_i}}.
    \label{eq:diagonal-perturbation}
\end{equation}
The relative difference between the proxy degree and the loopless expected degree is bounded by the same quantity.
Because \(\kappa_a\geq B_{aa}s_a\), this quantity is at most \(\max_i\theta_i/s_{g_i}\).
Both differences therefore vanish when the propensities remain bounded and every block propensity mass \(s_a\) grows.
\end{remark}

\section{Multiplicative block matrices}
\label{sec:factorization-lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Multiplicative block matrices produce community rays]
\label{lem:multiplicative-rays}
Let
\begin{equation}
    M=\diag(\xi)Z\mathsf{A} Z^\top\diag(\zeta),
    \label{eq:general-multiplicative}
\end{equation}
where \(\xi_i,\zeta_i>0\), \(\mathsf{A}\in\R^{K\times K}\), and \(h=\rank(M)\).
Suppose that \(M=UV^\top\) is a minimal exact factorization with
\(\rank(U)=\rank(V)=h\).
Then matrices \(C,E\in\R^{K\times h}\) exist such that
\begin{equation}
    u_i=\xi_i c_{g_i},
    \qquad
    v_i=\zeta_i e_{g_i},
    \label{eq:ray-rows}
\end{equation}
where lowercase letters denote rows of their corresponding matrices.
Consequently, whenever the community vectors involved are nonzero,
\begin{equation}
    \norm{u_i}=\xi_i\norm{c_{g_i}},
    \qquad
    \frac{u_i}{\norm{u_i}}
    =
    \frac{c_{g_i}}{\norm{c_{g_i}}},
    \label{eq:ray-norm-direction}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \cos(u_i,u_j)
    =
    \frac{c_{g_i}^\top c_{g_j}}
    {\norm{c_{g_i}}\norm{c_{g_j}}}.
    \label{eq:ray-cosine}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The column space of \(M\) is contained in the column space of \(\diag(\xi)Z\).
Minimality gives \(\col(U)=\col(M)\), so \(U=\diag(\xi)ZC\) for some \(C\).
Applying the same argument to \(M^\top\) gives \(V=\diag(\zeta)ZE\).
Equations~\eqref{eq:ray-rows}--\eqref{eq:ray-cosine} follow row by row.
\end{proof}

The lemma does not require \(M\) to be positive semidefinite or its factorization to be unique.
An invertible change of factorization changes the community vectors but preserves the multiplier \(\xi_i\) within each community.
A community vector \(c_a\) is zero exactly when row \(a\) of \(\mathsf{A}\) is zero, because the rows of \(Z^\top\diag(\zeta)\) are linearly independent.
Two communities have distinct normalized directions when their community vectors are nonzero and are not positive scalar multiples of one another.
Minimality, or an equivalent balancing condition, rules out canceling components in unused dimensions.

\section{Normalized spectral embeddings}
\label{sec:normalized-spectral}

Define the population normalized adjacency
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}
    =
    \mathcal{D}^{-1/2}\Omega\mathcal{D}^{-1/2}.
    \label{eq:normalized-adjacency}
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
    Q=\Theta^{1/2}Z\diag(s)^{-1/2}.
    \label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
Its columns are orthonormal because \(Q^\top Q=I_K\).
Define the block operator
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{B}=
    \diag(s)^{1/2}
    \diag(\kappa)^{-1/2}
    B
    \diag(\kappa)^{-1/2}
    \diag(s)^{1/2}.
    \label{eq:block-operator}
\end{equation}
Direct multiplication gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}=Q\mathcal{B}Q^\top.
    \label{eq:S-QHQ}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Degree controls radius in the population eigenspace]
\label{thm:spectral-rays}
Suppose that \(B\) has rank \(K\).
Let \(\mathcal{B}=Y\Lambda Y^\top\), with \(Y\) orthogonal.
The \(K\) nonzero eigenvectors of \(\mathcal{S}\) can be collected as \(U=QY\).
For node \(i\) in community \(a=g_i\),
\begin{equation}
    u_i=\sqrt{\frac{\theta_i}{s_a}}\,y_a,
    \label{eq:spectral-row}
\end{equation}
where \(y_a\) is row \(a\) of \(Y\).
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
    \norm{u_i}^2
    =
    \frac{\theta_i}{s_a}
    =
    \frac{\delta_i}{s_a\kappa_a},
    \qquad
    \frac{u_i}{\norm{u_i}}=y_a.
    \label{eq:spectral-norm-direction}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:S-QHQ} and \(Q^\top Q=I_K\) imply
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}(QY)=Q\mathcal{B}Y=QY\Lambda.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:spectral-row} follows from the definition of \(Q\).
The norm formula uses \(\norm{y_a}=1\).
\end{proof}

Using all \(K\) population eigenvectors, nodes in one community have cosine one, while nodes in different communities have cosine zero because the rows of \(Y\) are orthonormal.
If an embedding drops the trivial eigenvector or retains fewer dimensions, cross-community angles need not be right angles, but they remain functions of the two community labels.

\begin{corollary}[Degree recovery]
\label{cor:spectral-degree}
Within each community,
\begin{equation}
    \delta_i\propto\norm{u_i}^2.
\end{equation}
Across communities, recovery of \(\delta_i\) requires the block calibration factor \(s_a\kappa_a\).
\end{corollary}

Many matrix-factorization embeddings weight eigenvectors by eigenvalues.
Define
\begin{equation}
    x_i=e_i^\top U\abs{\Lambda}^{1/2},
    \label{eq:weighted-spectral-embedding}
\end{equation}
where \(\abs{\Lambda}\) applies absolute value to each eigenvalue.
Then
\begin{equation}
    x_i
    =
    \sqrt{\frac{\theta_i}{s_a}}\,
    y_a\abs{\Lambda}^{1/2}.
    \label{eq:weighted-row}
\end{equation}
Direction remains community-specific, and norm remains proportional to \(\sqrt{\theta_i}\) within a community.
If \(\mathcal{B}\succeq 0\), then
\begin{equation}
    \cos(x_i,x_j)
    =
    \frac{B_{ab}}{\sqrt{B_{aa}B_{bb}}},
    \qquad
    a=g_i,\quad b=g_j.
    \label{eq:block-affinity-cosine}
\end{equation}
For indefinite \(\mathcal{B}\), the same calculation replaces \(\mathcal{B}\) by \(Y\abs{\Lambda}Y^\top\), and the angle remains block-specific.

\section{Raw random-walk pairs}
\label{sec:raw-pairs}

Let
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}=\mathcal{D}^{-1}\Omega
    \label{eq:transition}
\end{equation}
be the population simple-random-walk transition matrix.
For \(a=g_i\) and \(b=g_j\),
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}_{ij}=\frac{\theta_jB_{ab}}{\kappa_a}.
    \label{eq:transition-entry}
\end{equation}
The induced block transition matrix is
\begin{equation}
    W_{ab}
    =
    \sum_{j:g_j=b}\mathcal{T}_{ij}
    =
    \frac{B_{ab}s_b}{\kappa_a}.
    \label{eq:block-transition}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Endpoint distribution at each lag]
\label{lem:lag-endpoint}
For every integer \(r\geq 1\),
\begin{equation}
    (\mathcal{T}^r)_{ij}
    =
    \frac{\theta_j}{s_b}(W^r)_{ab}.
    \label{eq:lag-endpoint}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:lag-endpoint} holds for \(r=1\) by Equation~\eqref{eq:block-transition}.
Assume that it holds at lag \(r\).
Grouping the intermediate node by community in
\((\mathcal{T}^{r+1})_{ij}=\sum_\ell(\mathcal{T}^r)_{i\ell}\mathcal{T}_{\ell j}\)
gives the formula at lag \(r+1\).
\end{proof}

Let \(\omega_1,\dots,\omega_L\geq 0\) be context-window weights with \(\sum_r \omega_r=1\), and define
\begin{equation}
    F=\sum_{r=1}^L \omega_rW^r.
    \label{eq:block-window}
\end{equation}
The stationary distribution is
\begin{equation}
    \varpi_i=\frac{\delta_i}{\vol(\Omega)}.
    \label{eq:stationary}
\end{equation}
Define the stationary joint probability of the ordered positive pair \((i,j)\) by
\begin{equation}
    J_{ij}
    =
    \varpi_i\sum_{r=1}^L \omega_r(\mathcal{T}^r)_{ij}.
    \label{eq:stationary-joint}
\end{equation}
For walks started from \(\varpi\), \(J\) is the expected positive-pair frequency, with \(\omega_r\) proportional to the number of lag-\(r\) pairs per walk.
If \(\mathcal{T}\) is irreducible, the empirical pair frequencies along a single long walk converge to \(J\) from any start, for a fixed finite window with limiting lag weights \(\omega_r\).
This time-average statement does not require aperiodicity; without irreducibility, the limit can depend on the starting component.

\begin{theorem}[Raw stationary pairs have radial-angular form]
\label{thm:raw-pair-rays}
For \(a=g_i\) and \(b=g_j\),
\begin{equation}
    J_{ij}
    =
    \theta_i\theta_jG_{ab},
    \qquad
    G_{ab}
    =
    \frac{\kappa_a}{\vol(\Omega)s_b}F_{ab}.
    \label{eq:J-factorization}
\end{equation}
The random walk is reversible, so \(G\) and \(J\) are symmetric.
In matrix form,
\begin{equation}
    J=\Theta ZGZ^\top\Theta.
    \label{eq:J-matrix}
\end{equation}
Every minimal exact factorization \(J=UV^\top\) therefore has
\begin{equation}
    u_i=\theta_i c_{g_i},
    \qquad
    v_i=\theta_i e_{g_i}.
    \label{eq:J-factor-rows}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substituting Equations~\eqref{eq:expected-degree},~\eqref{eq:lag-endpoint}, and~\eqref{eq:block-window} into Equation~\eqref{eq:stationary-joint} yields
\begin{equation}
    J_{ij}
    =
    \frac{\theta_i\kappa_a}{\vol(\Omega)}
    \frac{\theta_j}{s_b}F_{ab}.
\end{equation}
Reversibility gives \(J_{ij}=J_{ji}\).
Lemma~\ref{lem:multiplicative-rays} gives Equation~\eqref{eq:J-factor-rows}.
\end{proof}

Within community \(a\),
\begin{equation}
    \norm{u_i}
    =
    \theta_i\norm{c_a}
    =
    \frac{\delta_i}{\kappa_a}\norm{c_a}.
    \label{eq:raw-pair-norm}
\end{equation}
The raw stationary pair matrix therefore gives the strongest exact version of degree in radius and community in direction.
It describes pair probabilities before marginal normalization and the entrywise logarithm.

\section{Skip-gram with negative sampling}
\label{sec:sgns}

Let \(p^+_{ij}=J_{ij}\) be the positive-pair probability.
Suppose skip-gram with negative sampling (SGNS) draws \(k\) negative contexts per positive pair from
\begin{equation}
    q_j
    =
    \frac{\delta_j^\alpha}{Z_\alpha},
    \qquad
    Z_\alpha=\sum_\ell\delta_\ell^\alpha.
    \label{eq:negative-distribution}
\end{equation}
The expected negative weight for ordered pair \((i,j)\) is
\begin{equation}
    p^-_{ij}=k\varpi_iq_j.
    \label{eq:negative-weight}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Unconstrained SGNS score]
\label{thm:sgns-score}
For positive weights \(p^+_{ij}\) and \(p^-_{ij}\), the pairwise SGNS objective
\begin{equation}
    \ell_{ij}(x)
    =
    p^+_{ij}\log\sigma(x)
    +
    p^-_{ij}\log\sigma(-x)
    \label{eq:pairwise-sgns}
\end{equation}
has the unique maximizer
\begin{equation}
    x^\star_{ij}
    =
    \log\frac{p^+_{ij}}{p^-_{ij}}.
    \label{eq:pairwise-optimum}
\end{equation}
For the DCSBM walk and every block pair with \(F_{ab}>0\),
\begin{equation}
    x^\star_{ij}
    =
    H^{(\alpha)}_{ab}
    +(1-\alpha)\log\theta_j,
    \label{eq:dcsbm-sgns-score}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    H^{(\alpha)}_{ab}
    =
    \log F_{ab}
    -\log s_b
    -\alpha\log\kappa_b
    +\log Z_\alpha
    -\log k.
    \label{eq:sgns-block-score}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
    \ell'_{ij}(x)
    =
    \frac{p^+_{ij}-p^-_{ij}e^x}{1+e^x}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \ell''_{ij}(x)
    =
    -\frac{(p^+_{ij}+p^-_{ij})e^x}{(1+e^x)^2}<0.
\end{equation}
Thus \(e^{x^\star}=p^+_{ij}/p^-_{ij}\).
Substituting
\begin{equation}
    \sum_r \omega_r(\mathcal{T}^r)_{ij}
    =
    \frac{\theta_j}{s_b}F_{ab},
    \qquad
    \delta_j=\theta_j\kappa_b
\end{equation}
gives Equations~\eqref{eq:dcsbm-sgns-score} and~\eqref{eq:sgns-block-score}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Degree cancellation at \(\alpha=1\)]
\label{cor:alpha-one}
Assume \(F_{ab}>0\) for every block pair, so that the score matrix is finite.
If \(\alpha=1\), then
\begin{equation}
    x^\star_{ij}=H^{(1)}_{g_i g_j}.
    \label{eq:alpha-one-score}
\end{equation}
The score is block-constant and contains no node-specific propensity.
Every minimal exact factorization has center and context rows that depend only on community.
\end{corollary}

This cancellation is explicit in~\citet[Lemma~S9]{davison2024community}; here it follows for the finite population-walk proxy with ordered SGNS.

The \(\alpha=1\) result agrees with the NetMF target~\citep{qiu2018network}
\begin{equation}
    \log\left[
        \vol(\Omega)
        \left(\sum_r \omega_r\mathcal{T}^r\right)
        \mathcal{D}^{-1}
    \right]
    -\log k.
    \label{eq:netmf}
\end{equation}
Right multiplication by \(\mathcal{D}^{-1}\) already divides each conditional context probability by its stationary marginal.
A second subtraction of both stationary marginals would count this normalization twice.

\begin{corollary}[Additive context-degree term for \(\alpha\neq 1\)]
\label{cor:alpha-not-one}
Assume \(F_{ab}>0\) for every block pair, so that the score matrix is finite.
For ordered SGNS and \(\alpha\neq 1\), center-side rows of the score matrix remain block-constant.
Context columns receive the additive term \((1-\alpha)\log\theta_j\).
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
    X^\star
    =
    ZH^{(\alpha)}Z^\top
    +(1-\alpha)\ones(\log\theta)^\top
    \label{eq:sgns-score-matrix}
\end{equation}
has column space contained in \(\col(Z)\) and rank at most \(K\).
Every minimal exact factorization \(X^\star=UV^\top\) therefore has center rows \(u_i=c_{g_i}\), as at \(\alpha=1\), and context rows
\begin{equation}
    v_j=e_{g_j}+(1-\alpha)(\log\theta_j)\,w,
    \label{eq:sgns-context-rows}
\end{equation}
where \(e_b\) is shared within context block \(b\), and \(w\) is shared by all nodes.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:sgns-score-matrix} follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:sgns-score}.
Because \(\ones=Z\ones_K\),
\begin{equation}
    X^\star
    =
    Z\left[
        H^{(\alpha)}Z^\top
        +(1-\alpha)\ones_K(\log\theta)^\top
    \right].
\end{equation}
The column-space and rank claims follow.
Minimality gives \(\col(U)=\col(X^\star)\subseteq\col(Z)\), so \(U=ZC\), where \(C\) has full column rank.
Matching column \(j\) of \(X^\star=ZCV^\top\) gives
\(v_j^\top=C^{+}\bigl[h_{g_j}+(1-\alpha)(\log\theta_j)\ones_K\bigr]\),
where \(h_b\) is column \(b\) of \(H^{(\alpha)}\) and \(C^{+}\) is the left inverse of \(C\).
\end{proof}

Some implementations tie center and context scores or combine both orientations of each pair.
For a shared symmetric score, the pairwise optimum is
\begin{equation}
    x^{\mathrm{sym}}_{ij}
    =
    \log
    \frac{2J_{ij}}
    {k(\varpi_iq_j+\varpi_jq_i)}.
    \label{eq:symmetric-score}
\end{equation}
Under the DCSBM,
\begin{equation}
    x^{\mathrm{sym}}_{ij}
    =
    C_{ab}
    -
    \log\left(
        \theta_j^{\alpha-1}\kappa_a\kappa_b^\alpha
        +\theta_i^{\alpha-1}\kappa_b\kappa_a^\alpha
    \right),
    \label{eq:symmetric-dcsbm-score}
\end{equation}
where \(C_{ab}\) is block-specific.
The score is block-constant when \(\alpha=1\).
For \(\alpha\neq 1\), it is generally not multiplicatively separable; constant within-block propensities are a degenerate exception.

\begin{remark}[Zeros and finite counts]
If \(F_{ab}=0\), the unconstrained population score is \(-\infty\).
Finite-data methods must specify how they treat unobserved pairs, for example through truncation, smoothing, or a positive shifted-PMI convention.
For the loopless model of Section~\ref{sec:setup}, assume all loopless expected degrees are positive, and use the loopless walk's stationary distribution for positive pairs and for negative sampling at \(\alpha=1\).
At window one, \(J_{ii}=0\), so the diagonal score is \(-\infty\).
On off-diagonal pairs with positive weight, the scores equal Equation~\eqref{eq:alpha-one-score}, evaluated at window one, plus
\(\log[\vol(\Omega-\diag(\Omega))/\vol(\Omega)]-\log(1-\eta_i)-\log(1-\eta_j)\),
where \(\eta_i=\theta_iB_{g_ig_i}/\kappa_{g_i}<1\) is the ratio in Equation~\eqref{eq:diagonal-perturbation}.
\end{remark}

The pairwise optimum identifies dot products rather than the norm of either factor.
For ordered SGNS, the optimal score has slope \(1-\alpha\) against log context degree within each context block.
A law such as \(\norm{u_i}\propto d_i^{-\gamma}\) requires an additional argument about low-rank approximation, optimization, and factor balancing.

\section{Factorization gauge}
\label{sec:gauge}

The unconstrained SGNS loss depends on \(U,V\) only through \(UV^\top\).
For every invertible matrix \(R\),
\begin{equation}
    UV^\top
    =
    (UR)(VR^{-\top})^\top.
    \label{eq:gauge}
\end{equation}
Individual norms and angles of \(U\) are therefore not identified by the objective.
The invariance group contains all invertible transformations rather than only rotations.

Two restrictions make norm and angle claims well-defined.
One can tie the factors, \(U=V\), when the target admits the required positive-semidefinite factorization.
Alternatively, one can impose the balance condition
\begin{equation}
    U^\top U=V^\top V.
    \label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
For a rank-\(h\) score matrix \(M=P_{\mathrm{svd}}\Sigma Q_{\mathrm{svd}}^\top\), canonical balanced factors are
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{bal}}=P_{\mathrm{svd}}\Sigma^{1/2},
    \qquad
    V_{\mathrm{bal}}=Q_{\mathrm{svd}}\Sigma^{1/2}.
    \label{eq:balanced-factors}
\end{equation}
Equivalent minimal balanced factors differ only by an orthogonal transformation, which preserves row norms and angles~\citep[Proposition~S24]{davison2024community}.
Empirical norm-degree claims should use balanced factors or identify the optimizer-dependent gauge under study.

A direct consequence of the separable score objective is the following boundary for interpreting balanced norms.
\begin{proposition}[Loss convergence and balanced norms]
\label{prop:loss-norm-boundary}
For a finite score matrix \(M\), fix \(p^+_{ij}\geq0\) and \(p^-_{ij}>0\), and define
\begin{equation}
    L(M)=\sum_{ij}\left[p^+_{ij}\log(1+e^{-M_{ij}})
    +p^-_{ij}\log(1+e^{M_{ij}})\right].
    \label{eq:score-loss}
\end{equation}
If every \(p^+_{ij}>0\), the unrestricted loss has a unique finite minimizer \(M^\star_{ij}=\log(p^+_{ij}/p^-_{ij})\).
Every sequence approaching its minimum converges to \(M^\star\), and its canonical balanced row norms converge.
If some \(p^+_{ij}=0\), every sequence approaching the unrestricted infimum instead sends those scores to \(-\infty\); its total squared balanced center norm diverges.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For positive weights \(a,b\), the scalar loss has second derivative \((a+b)e^x/(1+e^x)^2>0\) and tends to infinity at both ends.
Thus \(L\) is strictly convex and coercive; compact sublevel sets and uniqueness imply convergence of every minimizing sequence.
The squared balanced row norms are the diagonal entries of \((MM^\top)^{1/2}\), a continuous matrix function.
When \(a=0<b\), the scalar infimum is zero, approached only as \(x\to-\infty\).
Separability forces this limit on every zero-positive pair in a minimizing sequence.
Finally,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \sum_i\norm{u_{i,\mathrm{bal}}(M)}^2
    &=\operatorname{tr}\!\left[(MM^\top)^{1/2}\right]\\
    &=\norm{M}_*\geq\norm{M}_{\mathrm F},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where \(\norm{M}_*\) is the sum of singular values.
\end{proof}

The divergence concerns the total norm, not necessarily every node.
It changes the score matrix and therefore persists after balancing; it is not merely factorization gauge freedom.
The proposition concerns unrestricted scores and asymptotically optimal loss.
With positive weights everywhere, a minimizer over matrices of rank at most \(r\) exists but need not be unique; neither case guarantees convergence of a rank-constrained optimizer.

Removing overall scale need not identify the curve either.
Write \(r_i(M)^2=[(MM^\top)^{1/2}]_{ii}\), \(\rho(M)^2=n^{-1}\sum_i r_i(M)^2\), and \(\widetilde r_i=r_i/\rho\) for \(\rho>0\).
\begin{proposition}[Different shapes at arbitrarily small loss gap]
\label{prop:normalized-shapes}
On a triangle with one pendant node, fix one-step contexts, uniform center exposure, stationary noise, five collision-allowing negatives, and dimension four.
For every \(\delta>0\), there are balanced embeddings with increasing, decreasing, and peaked norm-degree profiles, each within \(\delta\) of the same unrestricted loss infimum.
Their RMS radii are equal, and their RMS-normalized profiles are distinct.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The degrees are \((3,2,2,1)\), with weights \(p^+_{ij}=A_{ij}/(4d_i)\) and \(p^-_{ij}=5d_j/32\).
Keep edge scores at \(\log[8/(5d_id_j)]\) and set off-diagonal nonedge scores to \(-s\), where \(s=\max\{0,\log[(35/16)/\delta]\}\).
For each desired positive node profile \(h\), let \(\alpha_i=h_i^2/(\frac14\sum_jh_j^2)\) and set \(M_{ii}=-D\alpha_i\).
Choose one common \(D\) so that every diagonal is at most \(-s\) and strictly exceeds its off-diagonal absolute row sum in magnitude.
Then \(M\) is symmetric negative definite, so \((MM^\top)^{1/2}=-M\), \(r_i^2=D\alpha_i\), and \(\rho=\sqrt D\).
All edge terms are minimized; the remaining loss is at most \((35/16)e^{-s}\le\delta\), including the diagonals.
Assigning \(h\) by degree \(1,2,3\) as \((1,2,3)\), \((3,2,1)\), or \((1,3,1)\) yields the three shapes.
Balanced factors \(U=(-M)^{1/2}\), \(V=-U\) realize each score matrix in four dimensions.
\end{proof}

The common RMS can grow as the tolerance shrinks, but the normalized shapes remain different.
This is a statement about what the fixed objective permits, not which solution training selects, nor a guarantee about the larger rank-24 experiments.

\section{Perturbation of radius and direction}
\label{sec:finite-sample}

The population theorems describe exact rays.
The following deterministic lemma converts an assumed row-wise embedding error bound into bounds for radius and direction.
It does not supply a sampling error bound for a particular estimator.
For matrix-factorization embeddings, the population and estimated factors must first use a common gauge, for example the balanced convention in Equation~\eqref{eq:balanced-factors}; orthogonal alignment alone cannot remove a general invertible change of gauge.

\begin{lemma}[Stability of radius and direction]
\label{lem:stability}
Suppose that population rows satisfy \(u_i=\xi_i c_{g_i}\), and an estimated embedding obeys
\begin{equation}
    \norm{\widehat{u}_iO-u_i}\leq\varepsilon_i
    \label{eq:row-error}
\end{equation}
for a single orthogonal alignment \(O\) shared by all rows.
Let
\(\rho_i=\norm{u_i}=\xi_i\norm{c_{g_i}}>0\), and assume \(\varepsilon_i<\rho_i\).
Then
\begin{equation}
    \abs{\norm{\widehat{u}_i}-\rho_i}
    \leq
    \varepsilon_i
    \label{eq:norm-error}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \norm{
        \frac{\widehat{u}_iO}{\norm{\widehat{u}_i}}
        -
        \frac{c_{g_i}}{\norm{c_{g_i}}}
    }
    \leq
    \frac{2\varepsilon_i}{\rho_i}.
    \label{eq:direction-error}
\end{equation}
For two nodes,
\begin{equation}
    \abs{
        \cos(\widehat{u}_i,\widehat{u}_j)
        -
        \cos(c_{g_i},c_{g_j})
    }
    \leq
    \frac{2\varepsilon_i}{\rho_i}
    +
    \frac{2\varepsilon_j}{\rho_j}.
    \label{eq:cosine-error}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The reverse triangle inequality gives Equation~\eqref{eq:norm-error}.
For nonzero vectors \(x,y\),
\begin{equation}
    \norm{
        \frac{x}{\norm{x}}
        -
        \frac{y}{\norm{y}}
    }
    \leq
    \frac{2\norm{x-y}}{\norm{y}}.
\end{equation}
Apply this inequality with \(x=\widehat{u}_iO\) and \(y=u_i\) to obtain Equation~\eqref{eq:direction-error}.
Equation~\eqref{eq:cosine-error} follows by adding the two unit-vector errors.
\end{proof}

Let \(\Delta>0\) be the minimum Euclidean distance between distinct population directions.
Assignment to the nearest aligned population direction is exact whenever
\begin{equation}
    \max_i\frac{2\varepsilon_i}{\rho_i}<\frac{\Delta}{2}.
    \label{eq:direction-recovery}
\end{equation}
For the normalized-adjacency embedding, \(\rho_i\propto\sqrt{\theta_i}\) within each community.
At equal absolute error bounds \(\varepsilon_i\), Equation~\eqref{eq:direction-error} therefore gives a larger upper bound for low-propensity nodes.
The actual degree dependence of directional error also requires an estimator-specific analysis of \(\varepsilon_i\).
For minimal exact factorizations of the finite ordered SGNS score, the center rows are block-constant (Corollaries~\ref{cor:alpha-one} and~\ref{cor:alpha-not-one}), so their nonzero radii are constant within each community.
Any within-community degree dependence of this bound then enters through \(\varepsilon_i\).

\section{Relation to prior theory}
\label{sec:prior-theory}

\citet{qin2013regularized} derive the population ray structure of regularized spectral clustering under the DCSBM\@.
Theorem~\ref{thm:spectral-rays} gives the unregularized structure in notation that also supports the walk calculations.
The raw-adjacency population form \(u_i=\theta_i c_{g_i}\) underlies SCORE~\citep[Lemma~2.1]{jin2015fast}, where eigenvector ratios remove \(\theta_i\).
\citet{levy2014neural} derive the shifted-PMI interpretation of SGNS, and \citet{qiu2018network} identify the matrix targets for DeepWalk, LINE, PTE, and node2vec.

The graphon analysis of~\citet{davison2023asymptotics} gives a related degree-normalized logistic target [Eqs.~(16)--(17)].
Its nonedge-only negatives introduce a finite-density correction absent from our sampler (Remark~28).

\citet{kojaku2024network} connect a large-window linearization of the \(p=q=1\), \(\alpha=1\) score to the normalized Laplacian.
Their linearized matrix has the form
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{R}
    =
    D^{-1/2}\Gamma\Phi\Gamma^\top D^{-1/2},
    \label{eq:kojaku-congruence}
\end{equation}
where \(\Gamma\) contains normalized-Laplacian eigenvectors and \(\Phi\) transforms their eigenvalues.
Equation~\eqref{eq:kojaku-congruence} is a congruence rather than an ordinary eigendecomposition.
The columns \(U=D^{1/2}\Gamma\) solve
\begin{equation}
    D\widehat{R}U=U\Phi,
    \label{eq:kojaku-generalized}
\end{equation}
which is a generalized eigenproblem.
For a regular graph, \(D\) is proportional to the identity and the distinction disappears.
It may also become negligible in a planted-partition regime where degrees concentrate around one value.
Degree heterogeneity can affect the geometry of truncated embeddings.
With all \(n\) columns and \(\Phi\succeq 0\), the congruence factor \(D^{-1/2}\Gamma\Phi^{1/2}\) and the factor built from the eigendecomposition of \(\widehat{R}\) both satisfy \(XX^\top=\widehat{R}\).
They therefore differ by an orthogonal transformation and have the same row norms and, for nonzero rows, the same angles.
When degrees vary, keeping only \(d<n\) spectral components need not select the same subspace in the two constructions.

\citet{zhang2022exact} prove exact community recovery for matrix-factorization versions of DeepWalk and node2vec under stochastic block models and degree-corrected variants.
\citet{davison2024community} also establish asymptotic score and balanced-factor convergence (Theorems~2 and~3).
Their symmetrized negatives [Eq.~(S9), Appendix~F] yield the joint propensity dependence in Equation~\eqref{eq:symmetric-dcsbm-score}, whereas our ordered negatives give a context-only term.
These population results support our operator and gauge comparison but do not explain finite-training norm turnover.

\section{From population scores to trained norms}
\label{sec:finite-training-mechanism}

Corollary~\ref{cor:alpha-one} concerns walks on the population proxy \(\Omega\).
With stationary reference \(\varpi\), its window-averaged context KL is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{KL}^{\mathrm{pop}}_i
    =\sum_b F_{ab}\log\frac{F_{ab}\vol(\Omega)}{s_b\kappa_b},
    \qquad a=g_i,
    \label{eq:population-context-kl}
\end{equation}
using \(0\log0=0\).
This quantity is block-constant; for \(K=1\), it is zero and the unigram score is \(-\log k\).

A realized graph or finite corpus instead defines conditional contexts \(P_{ij}\).
For positive center shares \(c_i\) and negative proposal \(q_j\), the standard ordered SGNS weights are \(c_iP_{ij}\) and \(kc_iq_j\), giving \(x^\star_{ij}=\log[P_{ij}/(kq_j)]\) on their common positive support.
For a simple graph with positive degrees, window one, and \(q_j=\pi_j=d_j/\vol(G)\), this becomes
\begin{equation}
    x^\star_{ij}=\log\frac{\vol(G)}{kd_id_j}
    \quad\text{on edges}.
    \label{eq:realized-graph-score}
\end{equation}
Nonedges have score \(-\infty\), so no finite exact factorization represents the entire target.
Degree dependence therefore enters before optimization.
These weights allow negative collisions; literal Gensim skips proposals equal to the positive context.
Although \(c_i\) cancels from free pair scores at fixed \(P,q\), it still weights the rank-constrained loss, and changing exposure can also change \(q\).

For the same realized graph, let \(T_i\) be its one-step transition row.
Specificity relative to stationary noise is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{KL}_i
    =
    D_{\mathrm{KL}}(T_i\Vert\pi)
    =
    \log\frac{\vol(G)}{d_i}
    -
    \frac{1}{d_i}\sum_{j\in N(i)}\log d_j.
    \label{eq:graph-context-kl}
\end{equation}
In an uncorrelated configuration model, the neighbor average in Equation~\eqref{eq:graph-context-kl} changes little with \(d_i\), so specificity decreases approximately as \(-\log d_i\).
This is a realized-graph relation, not Equation~\eqref{eq:population-context-kl}.
The local KL approximation for centered, transformed embeddings~\citep{oyama2022norm} motivates a specificity hypothesis; it does not establish a decreasing raw or balanced norm at convergence.

Word2vec subsampling changes retained-token exposure and the conditional context distribution.
For token frequency \(p_i>\tfrac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}t\approx2.62t\), the Gensim form of the Mikolov retention probability is~\citep{mikolov2013distributed}
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{keep}_t(p_i)
    =
    \sqrt{\frac{t}{p_i}}+\frac{t}{p_i}.
    \label{eq:retention-probability}
\end{equation}
Below this cutoff the expression is at least one, so every token is kept.
The expected retained-center exposure is consequently
\begin{equation}
    m_i
    \propto
    p_i \operatorname{keep}_t(p_i)
    =
    \sqrt{tp_i}+t.
    \label{eq:retained-exposure}
\end{equation}
Exposure still increases with frequency.
Here \(m_i\) counts expected retained tokens, not realized positive pairs: deleting tokens before windowing also changes which contexts remain adjacent to each center.
Its log elasticity is one below the cutoff and
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\log m_i}{d\log p_i}
    =
    \frac{1}{2\left(1+\sqrt{t/p_i}\right)}
    \label{eq:retained-elasticity}
\end{equation}
above it, so it drops to about \(0.31\) at the cutoff and approaches \(1/2\) only as \(p_i/t\) grows.
Subsampling therefore weakens the marginal exposure gain without requiring a decreasing exposure count or a fixed ceiling.

Let a finite-training response \(\nu(\log m_i,\mathrm{KL}_i)\) approximate the learned norm in a fixed optimizer and gauge.
Its derivative along degree is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\nu}{d\log d_i}
    =
    \frac{\partial \nu}{\partial\log m_i}
    \frac{d\log m_i}{d\log d_i}
    +
    \frac{\partial \nu}{\partial \mathrm{KL}_i}
    \frac{d\mathrm{KL}_i}{d\log d_i}.
    \label{eq:two-force-derivative}
\end{equation}
If both response partial derivatives are positive, the two terms have opposite signs when exposure increases and specificity decreases with degree.
Their crossover describes an inverted-U within this response model; it does not assign either branch uniquely to transient training or to the converged target.

\section{Numerical experiments}
\label{sec:numerical-experiments}

\subsection{Intervention design}

We generated five heterogeneous configuration-model graphs with \(n=600\) nodes.
For each graph, we obtained a broad degree sequence from a Barab\'asi--Albert graph with attachment parameter three, sampled a configuration graph with that sequence, removed self-loops and parallel edges, and connected any remaining components.
Stationary frequency is proportional to degree in these undirected graphs.

The controlled experiment crosses two interventions.
The exposure intervention assigns an exact total of \(144{,}000\) positive pairs per condition and epoch, with center counts
\begin{equation}
    m_i(\beta)
    \propto
    \pi_i^\beta,
    \qquad
    \beta\in\left\{0,\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3},1\right\}.
    \label{eq:exposure-intervention}
\end{equation}
The specificity intervention replaces the one-step transition row by
\begin{equation}
    T_i^{(\lambda)}
    =
    (1-\lambda)T_i+\lambda q,
    \qquad
    q=\sum_i c_iT_i,
    \label{eq:specificity-intervention}
\end{equation}
where \(c_i=m_i/\sum_jm_j\) is the realized center share and \(\lambda\in\{0,1/3,2/3,1\}\).
Equation~\eqref{eq:specificity-intervention} preserves the intended marginal \(q\) before integer allocation, while reducing every row's KL divergence toward zero.
Generally \(q\neq\pi\), including at equal exposure.
Integer allocation can change the marginal and support; negatives follow the resulting empirical marginal.
Thus exposure also changes the noise distribution, and the interventions do not hold the score target fixed.

We trained 24-dimensional SGNS embeddings for four epochs with five negative samples per positive pair.
All conditions within a graph seed used the same initialization and optimizer seed.
We report raw center-vector norms and the canonical balanced norms defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:balanced-factors}; both give the same qualitative results.

\subsection{Norm curves under exposure and context interventions}

Figure~\ref{fig:controlled-turnover} compares these interventions.
With equal center exposure (\(\beta=0\)) and unmodified contexts, the mean raw norm decreases from \(3.507\) in the lowest degree bin to \(2.297\) in the highest, with Spearman correlation \(-0.758\).
With proportional exposure (\(\beta=1\)), it increases from \(2.496\) to \(4.802\), with correlation \(0.895\).
The intermediate intervention \(\beta=1/3\) produces an inverted-U with a peak norm of \(3.315\) near degree six; the peak is \(0.093\) above the lowest bin and \(0.361\) above the highest bin.
Thus turnover can occur while exposure strictly increases with degree.
Sublinear exposure does not guarantee it: at \(\beta=2/3\), the mean raw norm increases from \(2.861\) in the lowest bin to \(3.960\) in the highest, with Spearman correlation \(0.821\).

\subsection{Context mixing at equal exposure}

At equal exposure, increasing \(\lambda\) progressively flattens the norm-degree curve.
Full mixing gives a mean norm of \(0.9488\) in both the lowest and highest degree bins.
The total positive-center budget is fixed, but integer allocation can change the context marginal and hence the negative-sampling distribution.
The contrast therefore establishes sensitivity to context sampling at fixed exposure without isolating context specificity from noise changes.

A quadratic response surface in log exposure and scalar context KL was declared before the held-out runs.
This controlled fit uses the intended KL before allocation; the saved empirical KL can differ.
It failed at the strongest subsampling threshold: the observed curve peaked near degree \(7.73\), whereas the model predicted a monotone increase through the highest bin near degree \(16.04\).
A degree-bin-specific interpolation across \(\beta\), added after inspecting this failure, matched the held-out curve with pooled root-mean-square error \(0.013\) and placed the peak in the correct bin.
We treat this second fit as exploratory because its form was chosen after the global model failed.

\subsection{Wider windows with fixed exposure and negative sampling}
\label{sec:fixed-noise-windows}

We separately fit the exact expected loss with \(P_L=L^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^L T^r\), holding each graph, uniform center share \(c_i=1/600\), negative proposal \(q=\pi\), five collision-allowing negatives, and rank 24 fixed.
Two matched starts on each of five graphs were continued for up to 3000 additional iterations using score-preserving balanced restarts of L-BFGS.
From window five to ten, the highest-minus-preceding degree-bin norm gap weakens from \(-0.3258\pm0.0155\) to \(-0.2173\pm0.0123\); the corresponding KL gap changes from \(-0.2980\pm0.0142\) to \(-0.1303\pm0.0063\) (Fig.~\ref{fig:fixed-noise-windows}).
Both absolute contrasts weaken in every graph and start.
Norm declines normalized by the preceding bin also weaken, from \(12.31\%\) to \(10.03\%\), whereas relative KL declines increase; no common scale-free law follows.
Seven of ten window-five fits and all ten window-ten fits pass the predefined repeated loss, gradient, score, and norm stability checks; this does not certify global optimality.
Window ten has full positive support and still declining norms, so missing context pairs are not necessary for that decline.

The initial 500-iteration endpoints suggest monotone attenuation across windows one through ten, but this trend disappears under continuation; short-window results remain diagnostics rather than settled norm curves.
Two window-one fits lie within \(1.303\times10^{-6}\) and \(7.327\times10^{-7}\) of the unrestricted loss infimum, bounding their rank-24 objective suboptimality above, yet their highest-bin balanced norms change by \(5.01\%\) and \(1.13\%\) in the final block.
Thus nearly optimal loss can coexist with unsettled norm geometry beyond gauge ambiguity, consistent with the boundary in Proposition~\ref{prop:loss-norm-boundary}; these finite trajectories do not prove divergence or non-attainment at rank 24.

\subsection{Literal word2vec validation}

The original, uncorrected Gensim sweep trained word2vec models on paired random-walk corpora from the same five graph seeds.
Each corpus contains \(300n\) length-five walks with stationary starting nodes, and each model uses a 64-dimensional embedding, window size one, five negative samples, one worker, and five epochs.
The three subsampling thresholds \(t\in\{3\times10^{-4},10^{-3},3\times10^{-3}\}\) share the corpus, initialization seed, and training seed within each graph.

As subsampling weakens, the peak moves from the lowest degree bin at \(t=3\times10^{-4}\) to degree six at \(10^{-3}\) and degree \(7.73\) at \(3\times10^{-3}\) (Fig.~\ref{fig:gensim-turnover}).
The latter two conditions show inverted-U curves.
The rise from the lowest bin to the peak increases from \(0.372\) to \(0.569\), while the drop from the peak to the highest bin decreases in magnitude from \(0.537\) to \(0.238\).

Aggressive subsampling retains fewer tokens at a fixed epoch count.
That sweep also repeated training with eight, six, and five epochs, which approximately matches the expected retained-center totals across thresholds.

\subsection{Corrected no-subsampling and training-trajectory controls}

We repeated the fixed-five-epoch sweep with corrected repeated-visit counts, integer-token negative-table indexing, and compiled inner-product arithmetic, adding a condition with subsampling disabled.
All four conditions share the original corpus, initialization, and raw-token negative distribution within each graph.
Without subsampling, all five graphs have interior maxima in both raw and balanced norms; the pooled maximum lies near degree \(7.73\), with a raw-norm drop of \(0.244\) to the highest-degree bin.
Subsampling is therefore unnecessary for this turnover under the original training schedule.

To test dependence on training duration and learning-rate history, we trained forty continuous trajectories: five graphs, four subsampling settings, and two schedules.
The first schedule reproduces the original decay from \(0.025\) to \(0.0001\) over five epochs, then continues at \(0.0001\); the second uses a constant rate of \(0.0025\), the final rate used by the separate controlled trainer.
We recorded checkpoints after \(1,2,5,10,20\) epochs without restarting training or redefining either schedule.
All twenty five-epoch continuation checkpoints reproduce the corrected baseline factors exactly.
With subsampling disabled, the original schedule develops interior maxima by epoch two and retains them through epoch twenty in all five graphs.
Under the constant-rate alternative, no graph has an interior maximum at any recorded checkpoint; at epoch twenty all five binned curves are increasing, for both norm readouts (Fig.~\ref{fig:training-schedule}).
The weakest filtering setting, \(t=0.003\), shows the same schedule contrast.
At stronger filtering settings, interior maxima can appear, disappear, or reappear during training; the full trajectories and graph-level uncertainty are retained with the experiment outputs.

Neither loss reduction nor persistence of a binned maximum establishes convergence, and these tests do not identify why high-degree nodes follow different norm trajectories.
Embedding dimension and realized positive-pair budgets have not been matched between the controlled and Gensim pipelines.

\subsection{Prospective transfer to unseen graphs}

We chose a more flexible prospective predictor after the exploratory analyses above, then fixed its inputs, model class, hyperparameters, targets, and success criteria before training any target embedding.
External application of the Gensim retention law made two node-level inputs observable before training: the logarithm of the realized positive-pair count and the empirical context KL.
An extremely randomized tree regressor with 400 trees and minimum leaf size 40 mapped these two inputs to raw center norm (primary) and balanced norm (secondary)~\citep{geurts2006extremely}.
Leave-one-seed-out prediction within the five configuration-model calibration graphs gave raw-norm \(R^2=0.828\) and Spearman correlation \(0.932\).

The prospective targets comprised five direct Barab\'asi--Albert graphs with \(n=600\) and attachment parameter three~\citep{barabasi1999emergence}, five Holme--Kim clustered scale-free graphs with the same size and attachment parameter and triangle probability \(0.35\)~\citep{holme2002growing}, and the largest connected component of the NetScience coauthorship network, with 379 nodes and 914 edges~\citep{newman2006finding}.
NetScience used five independent walk and optimizer seeds on the same observed graph.
All conditions used \(300n\) stationary-start walks of length five, window size one, 64 dimensions, five negative samples, and five epochs.
A manifest recorded SHA-256 hashes of the protocol, calibration data, and all \(23{,}685\) node-level target predictions before target training; no outcome file existed at the freeze.

The four declared criteria all passed: three evaluate predictions, while the fourth describes observed peak ordering and permits ties (Fig.~\ref{fig:prospective-validation}).
The median absolute peak error was zero degree bins, eight of nine peaks were within one bin, pooled predicted-versus-observed node-norm Spearman correlation was \(0.881\), and the observed peak moved nondecreasingly with weaker subsampling in all three graph families.
Three boundary classifications differed: the strongest-subsampling direct Barab\'asi--Albert curve was decreasing rather than weakly unimodal, the weakest-subsampling clustered curve continued rising through its last bin, and the strongest-subsampling NetScience peak was two bins above its prediction.
The predictor also overestimated the absolute NetScience norms.
These misses limit the accuracy of the transferred predictor.

A post hoc degree-and-threshold baseline also passes all four criteria, but its median within-curve node-norm Spearman correlation is \(0.293\), versus \(0.842\) for exposure--KL.
The ranges, feature ablations, and corrected sampling comparisons are reported in Supplemental Material, Sec.~\ref{sm-sec:predictor-comparison} and Tables~\ref{sm-tab:prospective-baselines}--\ref{sm-tab:balanced-baselines}.
This ranking advantage, which does not extend consistently to peak localization, supports predictive value relative to the baseline rather than identification of a causal mechanism.

\section{Discussion}

The established population results provide a baseline for interpreting finite-training experiments.
Normalized spectral embeddings and raw random-walk pair probabilities preserve degree as a radial multiplier, with exponents \(1/2\) and \(1\), respectively.
For \(F_{ab}>0\) on all block pairs, unigram population degree cancellation concerns an idealized score matrix; realized contexts change that target, while rank constraints, factorization, and optimization determine the resulting norms.
Balancing removes factorization ambiguity, but nearly optimal loss alone can leave norm geometry unsettled and, in the exact construction, permit different shapes even after RMS normalization (Propositions~\ref{prop:loss-norm-boundary} and~\ref{prop:normalized-shapes}).
Structural interpretation therefore requires a specified graph-conditioned target and evidence about the accuracy and stability of the balanced norm readout; repeated-start agreement supports robustness without proving uniqueness.
An account of the empirical inverted-U must explain these additional stages; its complete explanation remains a future question.

This separation sharpens the qualitative account proposed for word and trajectory embeddings~\citep{schakel2015measuring,wilson2015controlled,murray2023unsupervised}.
The specified exposure and context interventions generate increasing, decreasing, and inverted-U curves even while exposure grows.
Within the response model, turnover occurs when the marginal norm gain from exposure becomes weaker than the norm loss associated with less specific contexts.
Standard word2vec subsampling makes retained-token exposure grow asymptotically as the square root of frequency, but this scaling does not establish the origin of the observed norm turnover.
The corrected controls show turnover without subsampling under the original annealing schedule, while a constant-rate alternative gives increasing unfiltered curves at twenty epochs.
Thus learning-rate history is an additional experimentally demonstrated dependence; the exposure--specificity account has not been established as a complete mechanism for the Gensim curves.
Frozen predictions from exposure and context KL preserved node-norm ordering and approximate peak movement across two unseen synthetic graph families and one empirical graph.

The numerical evidence has clear limits.
Most experiments use small graphs and one-step contexts; the fixed-noise window comparison additionally uses exact multi-step contexts and a separate expected-loss optimizer.
The five NetScience replicates resample walks and optimization on one graph rather than supplying five independent empirical networks.
External subsampling in the prospective test made every positive pair observable but differs from the usual internal word2vec implementation.
The raw center norm depends on the optimizer-selected gauge, although the balanced norm gives the same qualitative curves here.
Matching retained-center counts does not exactly match realized positive pairs after word2vec deletes tokens from a walk.
The global scalar response surface failed at the strongest held-out turnover, and the more accurate degree-bin interpolation was chosen after that failure.
The extremely randomized tree model was also chosen after the initial experiments, so only its evaluation on the three new target families is prospective.
Although it passed all declared criteria, it missed one peak by two bins, misclassified two boundary peaks, and overestimated the NetScience norm level.
The present evidence supports effects of the specified interventions and improved node-norm prediction relative to the degree-and-threshold baseline, without identifying a unique mechanism or a calibrated peak for natural sequence data.

Before transfer to natural sequences, the next tests should separate realized-graph sparsity from training dynamics and isolate learning-rate history at fixed graph and corpus.
The next test should freeze the model before applying it to natural text or mobility sequences, vary the window, dimension, and optimizer, and again predict peaks from realized exposure and context KL before target training.
It should also prespecify degree or frequency baselines (including threshold when varied), within-curve ranking criteria, and uncertainty across independent graphs or datasets.
General node2vec also requires a second-order state-space analysis on directed edges.

\section{Conclusion}

Random-walk embeddings do not have a single norm-degree law.
The normalized eigenspace, raw pair distribution, population SGNS score, and finite trained factors encode degree in different ways.
Under finite SGNS training, the exposure and context-sampling interventions produce an inverted-U even when exposure remains strictly increasing.
Hash-locked target predictions recover its approximate peak movement across new graph topologies, with identifiable boundary failures.
Interpreting embedding norms therefore requires naming the population operator, realized contexts, negative-sampling distribution, factorization gauge, and training procedure (including learning-rate history), and checking stability of the norm geometry itself.
